Skill or luck?

I rebuilt 1,606,386 World Series of Poker tournament entries to find out how long it takes for a good player's results to stop looking like a lucky one's. The answer is: longer than a career.

How many tournaments until you know you're good?

Each line is a player with a real, fixed edge. It shows how often their results would convince a statistician they're a winner, after a given number of events. A very good pro with a +20% edge needs about 25,000 events to be 80% sure, which is roughly 83 years of full-time play.

But skill is real

When players do cash, some reliably go deeper. I split each player's cashes in two and checked whether one half predicts the other. For real players it does, and more so with more data. With the results shuffled, it doesn't.

How big should your buy-in be?

The Kelly criterion picks the stake that grows a bankroll fastest. The catch: you never know your true edge, only your results. This calculator shrinks your record toward the average player by however much your sample size deserves, then sizes to that, using the real WSOP payout distribution.

How this was built

The data only shows winners

The source records who cashed, not who entered, so no player's profit can be known: their losing entries are invisible. I rebuilt each event's full field two independent ways, from the prize pool and from the number of places paid, and kept the 2,338 of 9,958 events where the two agree.

What to take with a grain of salt

  • Live events only. Online events pay a different share of the field and fail the cross-check.
  • Players are matched by name; two people with the same name merge.
  • The deep-run signal (r = 0.10 at 10+ cashes) may partly reflect which events people choose, not only how they play.
  • The calculator's prior, an average edge of -8% with a spread of ±25%, is an assumption, not a measurement.
